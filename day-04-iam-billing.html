What to remember

Domain 1 covers the permission structures, Domain 4 covers the money. Both are on this page.

  1. SCPs and permissions boundaries never grant anything. They only set a ceiling. A permission still has to be granted by an identity-based or resource-based policy underneath. → 05
  2. Effective permissions are the intersection of every layer. An action is allowed only if the identity policy, the boundary, and the SCP all permit it. → 05
  3. Spot is cheapest and can be taken away; Savings Plans are cheap and flexible. Match the purchase option to how interruptible the workload is. → 08
  4. Data in is generally free, data out is charged. That one asymmetry drives a surprising number of cost-optimisation answers. → 07
  5. Business support is the cheapest plan with 24/7 technical case support. Basic has no case support at all. → 11

01 Why permissions get hard

Attaching a policy to one user is easy. Attaching the right policy to four hundred users, as they join, move team and leave, is not.

Two ways to scale it
ApproachWhat happens
Policy per userEvery new hire needs a policy written. Every leaver needs one revoked. Permissions drift apart, nobody can answer "who can delete production", and the drift is invisible until an audit.
Policy per groupWrite the policy once for the job, then move people in and out. Joining a team is the permission change. One place to read, one place to fix.

This is the whole reason groups exist, and it is why Day 03 said to attach policies to groups rather than users. It is also where the limits start to show: groups cannot be nested, so a deep organisation runs out of expressiveness quickly.

02 RBAC and ABAC

Two models for deciding who gets what. One asks what your job is; the other asks what the resource is labelled.

Role-based vs attribute-based
 RBACABAC
Decides onThe role or group you are inTags on the identity and the resource
A new project meansA new group and a new policyA new tag value. No policy change.
Policy countGrows with the organisationStays roughly constant
Best forStable, well-known job functionsMany teams and projects, changing often

Tags

A tag is a key and a value attached to a resource: Project = apollo, Environment = production, CostCentre = 4021. They are free, and they do three jobs at once: drive ABAC policies, group costs in Cost Explorer, and find things later.

{
  "Effect": "Allow",
  "Action": "ec2:StartInstances",
  "Resource": "*",
  "Condition": {
    "StringEquals": {
      // the instance's tag must match the user's tag
      "aws:ResourceTag/Project": "${aws:PrincipalTag/Project}"
    }
  }
}

That single statement covers every project that will ever exist. Tag a user with their project, tag the instances with theirs, and the policy never needs editing again — which is exactly the argument for ABAC.

Caution ABAC lives or dies on tagging discipline

If resources go untagged, an ABAC policy silently grants nothing and people file tickets. Tag policies in Organizations, or a permission that requires a tag on creation, are how teams keep it honest.

03 AWS Organizations

One account stops being enough quickly. Organizations manages many accounts as a tree.

Why separate accounts at all

  • A blast radius. A mistake in the sandbox account cannot reach production, because they are different accounts.
  • One bill. Consolidated billing across every account, with volume discounts pooled.
  • Policy inheritance. Apply a rule once at an OU and every account under it obeys.
  • Clean cost attribution. Per-account costs need no tagging discipline to be meaningful.

Quotas worth knowing

Organizations limits
ThingLimit
Roots per organization1
OUs per organization2,000
OU nesting depth5 levels
SCPs per organization10,000
Maximum size of one SCP10,240 characters
SCPs attached to one entity10
Invitations per 24 hours20
Accounts, by default10, adjustable on request
Caution Three numbers in the class notes have moved

The notes record 1,000 OUs, 1,000 SCPs and 5,120 bytes per SCP. AWS's current quotas are 2,000 OUs, 10,000 SCPs and 10,240 characters. The 5,120 figure is now the limit for resource control policies, a different policy type, which is probably where the confusion came from. The table above follows AWS's live quota page, linked in the sources.

None of this is likely to be examined to the digit. The shapes worth carrying are: one root, five levels of nesting, and quotas that are mostly adjustable.

Organizations is reachable the usual four ways: Console, CLI, SDK and API.

04 Service control policies

An SCP is a ceiling on what an account may do. It never gives anyone anything.

Attach an SCP to the root, an OU or an account, and it limits every IAM user and role beneath it. The most common use is a guardrail: nobody in this organisation, however senior, may turn off CloudTrail or launch resources outside our approved Regions.

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Effect": "Deny",
    "Action": "*",
    "Resource": "*",
    "Condition": {
      "StringNotEquals": {
        "aws:RequestedRegion": ["eu-west-1", "eu-central-1"]
      }
    }
  }]
}

A deny like this cannot be overridden by any administrator in a member account, which is the point of putting it at the organisation level.

How SCPs behave
QuestionAnswer
Does it grant permissions?No. It only limits. The permission still has to be granted by an IAM policy.
Does it apply to the management account?No. SCPs do not restrict the organisation's management account, which is one reason not to run workloads there.
Does it apply to the root user?Yes, in member accounts. This is the one thing that can constrain a member account's root user.
What does an action need?To be allowed in both the SCP and an IAM policy. Either one saying no is enough.

05 Layered permissions

Several policy types can apply to one request. Two of them only subtract, and knowing which is the whole game.

Grants against ceilings
TypeAttached toGrants?Limits?
Identity-based policyUser, group, roleYesYes, via explicit deny
Resource-based policyThe resourceYesYes, via explicit deny
Permissions boundaryOne user or roleNoYes
Service control policyRoot, OU or accountNoYes
Danger The single most misread idea here

A permissions boundary that permits s3:* does not give anybody S3 access. It says: this identity may never exceed S3, whatever its policies say. Attach that boundary to a user with no other policy and they can do nothing at all.

Same for an SCP. Both are ceilings. Something underneath still has to do the granting.

Working out the effective permission

An action is allowed only when every applicable layer permits it. In effect, the permission is the intersection:

A worked example
LayerSaysRunning result
SCP on the OUAllow everything except iam:*Everything but IAM
Permissions boundaryAllow s3:* and ec2:*S3 and EC2 only
Identity policyAllow s3:* and rds:*S3 only

RDS was granted but sits outside the boundary. EC2 is inside the boundary but was never granted. Only S3 survives all three. And an explicit deny in any layer would end it immediately, as on Day 03.

Note Boundary or SCP?

A permissions boundary targets one identity. Its classic use is safe delegation: let a team lead create IAM users, but bound them so the users they create can never exceed what the lead has.

An SCP targets whole accounts. Its classic use is organisation-wide guardrails that no local administrator can lift.

06 Control Tower

Organizations gives you the parts. Control Tower assembles them into a working multi-account setup.

  • Sets up a landing zone: a multi-account environment built to AWS's own recommended pattern.
  • Creates the usual accounts for you, including log archive and audit.
  • Applies controls (formerly "guardrails") — preventive ones implemented as SCPs, detective ones as AWS Config rules.
  • Provides an account factory so new accounts come out pre-configured rather than hand-built.
  • Gives a dashboard showing which accounts are drifting out of compliance.

In a sentence: Organizations is the mechanism, Control Tower is the opinionated setup on top of it. If a question describes a company wanting a governed multi-account environment without designing one from scratch, Control Tower is the answer.

07 How AWS charges

Three things are metered, and one asymmetry causes most surprise bills.

What is metered
DimensionCharged as
ComputePer hour or per second, while it runs. Stop the instance and compute charges stop.
StoragePer GB per month, whether or not you touch it. An EBS volume attached to a stopped instance still costs.
Data transferInbound is generally free. Outbound to the internet is charged.
Caution The asymmetry is deliberate

Getting data into AWS costs nothing; getting it out costs per gigabyte. That shapes real architecture: it is why CloudFront in front of S3 saves money, why cross-AZ chatter shows up on bills, and why "download everything back out nightly" is an expensive design. When a cost question involves lots of egress, look for a caching or endpoint answer.

Four ways the price comes down

  • Pay as you go — no commitment, but the highest unit price.
  • Pay less when you reserve — commit to one or three years for a substantial discount.
  • Pay less by using more — volume tiers on S3, EBS and EFS mean the marginal GB gets cheaper.
  • Pay less as AWS grows — economies of scale get passed on; the class noted 134 price reductions between 2006 and 2023.

Very large customers can also negotiate custom pricing, which is not something the exam tests.

08 Four ways to buy compute

Same instance, four price tags. The difference is what you promise and what you risk.

Caution Spot is not "cheap On-Demand"

AWS reclaims Spot capacity when it needs it, giving about two minutes of warning. That makes Spot excellent for batch jobs, rendering, CI runners and anything that can restart, and unusable for a database or a checkout service.

Exam shorthand: fault-tolerant, interruptible, stateless → Spot. Steady baseline → Savings Plans or Reserved. Unpredictable or short-lived → On-Demand.

09 The free tier

The free tier changed in July 2025, and most study material still describes the old one.

Old and new
 Accounts before 15 July 2025Accounts after
ShapeTwelve months of per-service free allowancesCredits you spend on anything
AmountService-by-service quotas$100 on signup, up to $100 more earned — $200 maximum
Duration12 months6 months, or until credits run out
At the endReverts to pay-as-you-goA Free plan account closes; a Paid plan keeps billing

Both models keep the always free tier: more than 30 services with permanent monthly allowances that never expire, for any account. There are also short-term trials on individual services.

Good news The class notes are current here

"$200 for 6 months" and "30+ always free" match the new model exactly. Worth knowing the twelve-month version existed, since older practice questions still assume it.

10 Watching the bill

One estimating tool, one comparison method, and three tools for after the money is spent.

Before you build

Planning tools
ToolFor
Pricing CalculatorModel an architecture and get an estimate before building it.
Total Cost of OwnershipA financial estimate covering direct and indirect costs, used to compare cloud against on-premises. The indirect half — floor space, power, cooling, staff time, hardware refresh — is what makes on-premises look cheaper than it is.

After you build

AWS Budgets

  • Set a threshold, get alerted when spend or usage crosses it
  • Can alert on forecast, not just actuals
  • The one to name when a question says "notify before we overspend"

Cost Explorer

  • Visualise and break down spend over time
  • Filter by service, account, tag
  • For "why did the bill go up"

Cost and Usage Report

  • The most granular data available, as CSV into S3
  • Line-item level, for your own analysis
  • For finance teams and chargeback

Consolidated billing

With Organizations, every account's usage rolls up to the management account. Two consequences:

  • One bill for the whole organisation instead of many.
  • Volume discounts arrive sooner, because usage is pooled across accounts. Ten accounts each storing a little hit the next S3 pricing tier together, which none would reach alone.

11 Support plans

Four tiers in the class slide. The dividing line that matters is whether you can open a technical case at all.

Response time by severity
PlanCriticalUrgentHighNormalLow
BasicNo case support. Documentation, forums and health dashboard only.
Developer business hours———12 hours24 hours
Business 24/7—1 hour4 hours12 hours24 hours
Enterprise 24/715 minutes1 hour4 hours12 hours24 hours
Note There is a fifth plan the slide leaves out

AWS also sells Enterprise On-Ramp, between Business and Enterprise, with a 30-minute critical response. The slide's four are the ones usually asked about, but do not be thrown if a question lists five.

Good news The exam shorthand

Business is the cheapest plan with 24/7 technical support and third-party software help — the usual answer for a production workload. Enterprise adds a technical account manager and the 15-minute critical response, for business-critical systems. Developer is for experimenting. Basic cannot open a technical case.

12 Lab: exploring IAM

AWS Academy guided lab. Three users, three groups, three job functions — the group argument from section 01, made concrete.

The lab's business scenario
UserIn groupGets
user-1S3-SupportRead-only access to Amazon S3
user-2EC2-SupportRead-only access to Amazon EC2
user-3EC2-AdminView, start and stop EC2 instances

What the lab walks through

  1. Inspect the pre-created users and notice each starts in no group, with a console password under Security credentials.
  2. Look at the three groups and their attached policies — AmazonEC2ReadOnlyAccess and AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess are AWS managed policies, prebuilt and maintained by AWS.
  3. Read the EC2-Admin policy as JSON and see it grant Describe, StartInstances and StopInstances — a custom policy rather than a managed one.
  4. Add each user to the group matching their job function.
  5. Sign in as each user in turn and confirm they can do exactly what the group allows, and nothing else.
Good news Three things the lab is really teaching

Permissions came from group membership, not from the user. No policy was ever attached to a user directly — which is section 01's argument demonstrated rather than asserted.

Managed policies update themselves. When AWS adds an EC2 read action, AmazonEC2ReadOnlyAccess picks it up and every attached group gets it. A copied inline policy would not.

Signing in as the user is the only real test. Reading a policy and believing it does what you think is how over-permissive access survives review.
